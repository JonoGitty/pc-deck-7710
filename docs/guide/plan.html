Chapter 1

Plan the build. Pick a display first — it fixes the grid everything else is laid out on.

There are two decisions here and everything downstream follows from them: which panel, and whether this deck is going in a car. Take the first one seriously: the panel decides the logical grid, the number of brightness levels, and whether thin bright detail survives at all.

Two corrections already made the hard way.

It must be the original ESP32, not the S3 — the S3 has no Bluetooth Classic, so no A2DP, so no audio from a phone. Espressif closed that request “Won't Do”.

The 8.8″ bar LCD does not fit a 1-DIN slot: about 217 mm wide against a 180 mm fascia. There is a colour panel that fits — a 4.58″ bar IPS at 118.8 × 41.57 mm, which maps onto the grid exactly at 192 × 64 logical dots times five — but it needs a parallel RGB bus the original ESP32 does not have, so colour is a two-chip build.

Build the bench tier first, whatever you are aiming at

A panel, an ESP32 and a USB cable on a desk is about £32 and it plays music from your phone. Every chapter up to Flash it applies to it unchanged, and it turns the car build from one long project with a single moment of truth into two short ones. If something is wrong with your soldering or your wiring you would much rather find out on a desk than upside down under a dashboard.

Which grid you get

SSD1322 → 256×64 with four usable levels. GP1294AI → 256×48, 1-bit. The legacy PC deck → 192×48. The renderer lays out for each of them; you do not have to.

The level-centre rule

Level n renders at shade (n+0.5)/4, so 0.375, 0.625 and 0.875 are solid fields and 0.25 or 0.5 are 50/50 checkerboards. A checkerboard over a third of the panel beats everything else for attention.

Level 4 is spoken for

It is the clipping indicator and renders red. Movies and animations use 0–3 only, which is why the deck has five levels and four of them are yours.
