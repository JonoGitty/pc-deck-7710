Into the car. This is the chapter where the consequences change.

⚠️ Read SAFETY.md before this chapter, not after it. Everything up to now happened on a desk from USB. From here you are wiring a device you built into a vehicle's electrical system, next to circuits that are not yours — airbags, immobiliser, alternator. If you build one you are the manufacturer, and every consequence is yours.

The order

  1. Battery negative off first. Then wait — some cars want a few minutes before anything is disturbed, and the airbag module is the reason.
  2. The cage goes in and stays in. Bend the tabs outward from inside the dash, and only the tabs that reach metal. The rest bend back and rattle for the life of the car.
  3. Meter the harness. ISO 10487 standardises the connector, not the pinout: A4 (permanent) and A7 (ignition) get swapped by manufacturers. Two minutes with a meter against a known-good earth.
  4. Connect ISO A and ISO B, then the aerial, then the amplifier's own fused feed — taken from the battery side, not from the deck's supply.
  5. One ground point. The amplifier and the deck share it. Two grounds is what alternator whine is.
  6. Slide it home until both spring tabs click, and fit the rear support strap if the car has a bracket for one. A head unit held only by its face works its solder joints loose over a few thousand miles.

The three wires that are not power

A7 — switched live

Tells the deck the ignition is on. It goes through an opto-isolator, not straight to a GPIO: the car's 12 V rail is a hostile place and a load dump does not respect a 3.3 V input.

A6 — illumination

The dash dimmer. The deck reads it to drop panel brightness at night, which is the difference between a period-correct head unit and a torch in your peripheral vision.

A4 — permanent live

Keeps presets, pairings and the radio region across ignition cycles. Its current draw is why deckctl logs reports the deep-sleep state — a deck that flattens a battery over a fortnight is a deck nobody keeps.

Where the amplifier physically goes

This is car-specific and, in a small roadster, genuinely hard — chapter 2 answers it per car. A 2-DIN aperture is the way out: deck in the top half, amplifier board in the bottom, front end and amp in one box, which is exactly what a factory head unit is. If you reused the donor's amplifier (chapter 5) the question does not arise: it is already inside the deck, bolted to the chassis that is its heatsink.
